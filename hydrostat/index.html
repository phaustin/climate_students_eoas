Hydrostatic balance

In order to calculate the optical depth we need n, the number concentration of molecules at a particular height in the atmosphere. This means that we need to know the density as a function of height.

At large scales it is a very good approximation to assume hydrostatic balance, which is the statement that the vertical pressure differential across a layer provides exactly the amount of force necessary to balance gravity, so the atmosphere is not rising or sinking:

In symbols, the balance shown by Figure 1 implies that:

dp=−ρgdzdp = - \rho g dzdp=−ρgdz
(1)

Question: is this the same pressure p as the local pressure given by the equation of state: p=ρRdTp=\rho R_d Tp=ρRd​T?

Scale Heights¶

In Chapter 1 (p. 8) Roland introduces the idea of a characteristic length scale for the variation of pressure in the atmosphere, and on page 14 he defines the equation of state for dry air. To get weighting functions for our sounding retrievals we need to now the optical depth τ\tauτ vs. height, which means that we need to know the gas density ρ\rhoρ as a function of height. Below I show how to get ρ(z)\rho(z)ρ(z) for typical atmospheric conditions.

p=p0exp⁡(−zHp)p = p_0 \exp \left ( \frac{-z}{H_p} \right )p=p0​exp(Hp​−z​)
(2)

In words -- HpH_pHp​ gives the height at which gravity wins out over the kinetic energy of the air molecules -- warmer atmospheres take longer to decrease their pressure by a factor of exp⁡(−Hp/Hp)=exp⁡(−1)\exp(-H_p/H_p) = \exp(-1)exp(−Hp​/Hp​)=exp(−1) (the e-folding height) because the air molecules have more energy to travel higher.

    (3)

    where Hp=RdT/gH_p=R_d T/gHp​=Rd​T/g is the pressure scale height.

    dln⁡p=−dzHpd\ln p = - \frac{dz }{H_p}dlnp=−Hp​dz​

    ∫p0p ⁣ dln⁡p=−∫0z ⁣1Hpdz′\int_{p_0}^{p}\!\,d \ln p = - \int_{0 }^{z}\!\frac{1}{H_p} dz^\prime∫p0​p​dlnp=−∫0z​Hp​1​dz′

    Since temperature (and gravity) are changing with height, we need to know profiles of g(z) and T(z) to go any further. If we don’t have those profiles, then a useful approximation is to define a vertically averaged HpH_pHp​, since the average involves a large mass of air (about 10 metric tons/cubic meter), it will vary more slowly than the temperature at any one level on any given day, and we can still get a useful profile of pressure vs. height without having a specific sounding:

    1Hp‾=(1Hp)‾=∫0z ⁣1Hpdz′z−0\frac{ 1}{\overline{H_p}} = \overline{ \left ( \frac{1 }{H_p} \right )} = \frac{\int_{0 }^{z}\!\frac{1}{H_p} dz^\prime }{z-0}Hp​​1​=(Hp​1​)​=z−0∫0z​Hp​1​dz′​

    ∫p0p ⁣ dln⁡p=−∫0z ⁣1Hpdz′=−zHp‾\int_{p_0}^{p}\!\,d \ln p = - \int_{0 }^{z}\!\frac{1}{H_p} dz^\prime = -\frac{z}{\overline{H_p}}∫p0​p​dlnp=−∫0z​Hp​1​dz′=−Hp​​z​
    (7)

    and integrating the left-hand side:

    ln⁡p/p0=−zHp‾\ln p/p_0 = - \frac{z }{\overline{H_p}}lnp/p0​=−Hp​​z​

    Taking exp of both sides gives the pressure with height:

    p=p0exp⁡(−zH‾p)p = p_0 \exp \left ( - \frac{z }{\overline H_p} \right )p=p0​exp(−Hp​z​)
    (9)

    Values for Hp‾\overline{H_p}Hp​​ are relatively constant for a particular climate regime, like midlatitude winter.
